Stream sells chat, video, activity feed, and moderation APIs. Developers buy first; enterprise sales picks up accounts once usage grows, which is how eBay, Patreon, Robinhood, and Strava ended up on the logo wall. What makes Stream worth studying is that nearly the whole playbook is legible from the outside, and it is the most complete example of a dev-tool company marketing to both of its readers: the developer and the developer's agent.

## Pricing that recruits instead of gates

The Build plan is $0 for 1,000 monthly active users with no credit card. Start is $399 a month on annual billing, Elevate $599, both self-serve. Only Enterprise says "contact sales", and even the overage rate is published: $0.09 per extra MAU. On top of that sits the Maker Account, free paid-tier access for teams of five or fewer with under $100k in funding and under $10k in monthly revenue. The return is a pipeline of small companies that grow into paid plans, plus a steady supply of "Maker projects we love" posts that become case-study material. The free floor isn't generosity; it's sourcing.

## A content engine with a number in every headline

Per-platform tutorials (React, iOS, Android, Flutter, Expo), a "build a Slack clone" series, sample repos, and a YouTube channel. Stream's own developer-marketing post says the chat tour takes "less than three minutes" and that they give away "90 to 95%" of what they know. The case studies all lead with one hard metric: eBay runs 1B+ conversations and 277M+ moderated items a month (that one sits on the homepage), Subito migrated 10M+ users without downtime, a gaming publisher moved 750M+ players off Community Sift in six months, Care.com cut bugs by up to 90%. Comparison content goes straight at the category leader: the "Sendbird alternatives" listicle ranks Stream first among eight vendors with a 24-row feature table, and it works because Sendbird's name carries real search volume. Thirteen vertical pages (dating, fintech, healthcare, marketplaces) each carry their own proof points.

## The glossary, and what AI answers did to it

The most-copied piece is the technical glossary: roughly 200 entries defining the words engineers type into Google (codec, bitrate, RTSP, RCS chat), cross-linked, with almost no CTAs. It is still Stream's biggest content asset, 12,611 of the site's 43,645 monthly US organic visits as of October 2026, but the curve has turned. Glossary traffic grew from 5,841 visits in March 2024 to a peak of 25,577 in January 2025. Since then AI Overviews went from covering none of its keywords to 95%, visits per ranking keyword fell from 6.9 to 2.2, and traffic has roughly halved from the peak even though the glossary now ranks for nearly twice as many keywords as it did then. For a while, shipping pages faster than clicks decayed kept the total growing. That phase is ending. What survives is quieter: those short, quotable definitions are exactly what answer engines lift, which puts Stream's name in the AI answer with no click recorded anywhere.

## The agent wedge

Vision Agents is Stream's open-source framework for voice and video AI agents: Apache 2.0, about 8,000 GitHub stars, and deliberately model-neutral, plugging into OpenAI, Gemini, xAI, Deepgram, ElevenLabs, and Roboflow. The framework is free. The edge network it runs on is the paid product, and new users get 333,000 free participant minutes a month to feel the difference. Around it, Stream ships docs for streaming LLM output into chat UIs, a LangChain integration, and an agent-skills repo installed with `getstream skills` into Claude Code. The homepage shows a copyable prompt: "build a social messaging app: getstream.io/SKILL.md". Most companies bury their agent surfaces three clicks deep in the docs. Stream treats the agent as a front-door visitor.

## What to copy, what to skip

Copy the shape: prices a buyer (or an agent) can read without a call, one metric per case study headline, comparison pages aimed at a leader whose name people actually search, and agent onboarding on the homepage rather than buried in the docs. Be careful with the glossary: the structure is right, but the 2023 click math is gone, so write definitions to be quoted and attach something an AI answer can't replicate. And note what Stream's clone tutorials actually earn: GitHub and YouTube reach, not Google traffic. If your category's search pool is small, that's the channel they belong to.
