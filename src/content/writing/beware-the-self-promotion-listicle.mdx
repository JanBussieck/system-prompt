I recently did some research into Ramp's Reddit strategy, since I noticed that they maintained a separate company [subreddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/Rampplatform/) where they regurgitate their website content, presumably in order to feed that info to LLMs in the form of their favorite information sources.
I refused to belief a tactic this brazen actually worked and sampled a number of queries in Claude to see whether they were picked up and how Claude treats this subreddit as a source.

This led to something curious, I specifically asked about the [best clinical-trial financial-management software](https://www.reddit.com/r/RampPlatform/comments/1ut02y6/7_best_clinical_trial_financial_management/), it pulled facts straight from Ramp's own "7 best" listicle, any mention of Ramp itself, however, was conspicuously absent. 
Not even a mention in the long list of providers and tools, though, of course, Ramp had ostensibly ranked itself as number one.

Claude (Opus 5) seemed to have deliberately thrown out Ramp.


## How Ramp disqualified itself, twice

The clarifying conversation that followed surfaced two own goals.

The first was self-placement, Ramp ranked itself number one in a category it doesn't really belong to, since it's a general spend-management platform rather than clinical-trial-specific software. An LLM cross-references. A number one self-ranking with no supporting evidence, authored by a company that isn't a specialist in the specialist category, is recognized by the model as what it is, blatant and self promotion... and demoted accordingly.
Interestingly, it kept the other bits of the content that it judged useful and fair.

The second was self-distribution dressed up as corroboration. The Reddit skin-suit tactic, seems to have been counterproductive. It was clear to Claude that this wasn't an independent source agreeing with Ramp (the same listicle was published on Ramp's blog).

Ramp put in the work to put out a market survey and put itself on the map; its competitors got recommended partly on the strength of Ramp's own article, only to ensure it stays invisible in the conversation.

## The counterintuitive part

The implication runs backwards from how most marketing teams think. 
Being even-handed doesn't weaken your self-recommendation. 
It's the only thing that makes it survivable. When you disclose that you built one of the tools, treat competitors fairly, and back every claim, the model has no reason to discount you, so your honest case for yourself actually lands.

There's a bigger prize than topping your own ranking. An evenhanded comparison with disclosed authorship tends to become the source a model cites for the entire category, which makes you the reference rather than an entrant. [Being the default an LLM reaches for](/writing/becoming-the-default-choice) applies to comparison content too, and the citation goes to the fairest, most falsifiable page rather than the most flattering one.

## What good looks like

The cleanest example isn't in dev tools. It's Wise, which built a brand on showing you the real, total cost of a transfer with competitors' fees included: the actual numbers, laid side by side, even where the gap is narrow. The comparison is the product's honesty made visible, and you trust the figure precisely because it sits next to the figures it could have hidden.

Contrast that with the reflex. While writing this I opened a handful of comparison pages from companies with genuine reputations for candor. Several still ranked themselves first, gave themselves zero cons, and handed each competitor exactly one. A reputation for honesty and an honest page are different things, and the model only ever sees the page.

I'll indict my own side too. We publish a "Best Web-to-Print Editor SDKs" guide at IMG.LY. It's better than the average vendor listicle, with five real competitors, Adobe InDesign Server among them, each with a legible trade-off. And it would still get discounted. Our product is listed first with the most coverage, it's the only entry with no cons, the criteria happen to flatter our strengths, the conclusion crowns us "the most complete package," and nowhere does it admit we wrote it. Structurally, it fails the same test Ramp's did. Knowing the pattern and being clean of it are not the same thing.

## Criteria for an LLM-credible listicle

If you want your comparison content mined *and* trusted, not just mined:

1. Disclose authorship and bias up front. "We build CE.SDK; read our conclusion accordingly." Disclosure is a credibility multiplier, not a confession.
2. Give your own product honest cons, including a "when *not* to choose us" section that names the better-fit competitor per scenario.
3. Order by use case or alphabetically. Never self-first.
4. State neutral criteria before the list and apply them symmetrically, with the same fields and the same depth for every entry, yours included.
5. Be specific and falsifiable. Pricing bands, benchmarks, feature matrices, customer quotes, dates. Specificity earns citations; verdicts don't.
6. Replace superlatives with conditional recommendations. Not "the most complete," but "best if you need X; if you need Y, pick Z."
7. Keep it current, with a visible last-updated date.

## Audit your own before an LLM does

Reading your comparison page charitably is the trap, because you know what you meant. An LLM doesn't, and it's the reader that matters now. So hand the job to one. Paste this into Claude Code (pointed at the page or the file) or any capable model, and let it grade you the way the systems answering "what's the best X?" actually will.

```text
You are auditing a vendor-authored comparison article ("listicle") the way an
LLM does when answering a user's "what's the best X?" question. Predict whether
an AI assistant would TRUST AND CITE this page, or MINE ITS FACTS AND DISCOUNT
its recommendation. Be adversarial. Read it as a skeptic, not as the author.

INPUTS
- Article: <URL or pasted text>
- Publisher (author's company): <YOUR COMPANY>
- Author's product in the list: <YOUR PRODUCT>

Score each criterion PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL. Quote the exact evidence and say how
an LLM would read it.
1. Disclosure. Does it state up front that the author builds one of the tools?
2. Honest cons. Does the author's own product get real cons, including a
   "when NOT to choose us" that names a better-fit competitor per scenario?
3. Ordering. Is it ordered by use case or alphabetically, not author-first?
4. Symmetric criteria. Are neutral criteria stated before the list and applied
   with the same fields and depth to every entry, the author's included?
5. Falsifiability. Are claims specific and checkable (pricing bands, benchmarks,
   feature matrices, customer quotes, dates) rather than adjectives?
6. Conditional recommendations. Are superlatives ("the most complete") replaced
   with use-case-tied picks ("best if X; if you need Y, choose Z")?
7. Freshness. Is there a visible last-updated date?

Then flag the two self-disqualifiers:
- SELF-PLACEMENT: is the author's product ranked #1, given the most coverage, or
  the only entry with no cons?
- SELF-DISTRIBUTION: are there identical-title copies of this piece on the
  company's own channels (its subreddit, Medium, etc.) posing as independent
  corroboration? (These carry no extra weight. An LLM treats them as one source.)

OUTPUT
- A table: criterion -> verdict -> evidence quote.
- Verdict: would an LLM cite this as a neutral source, or mine-and-discount it?
  One line of reasoning.
- The 3 highest-leverage fixes, each as a concrete before/after edit.
- A drafted "When NOT to choose <YOUR PRODUCT>" section if one is missing.
```

Run it on your own best comparison page first. If the verdict is "mine-and-discount," you've just found why you're the raw material for a competitor's recommendation instead of the recommendation.

## The reframe

A listicle isn't a place to win. It's a place to be *useful*, and useful enough that the system answering the world's "what's the best X?" questions decides your page is worth quoting. Be upfront about when you're the wrong choice. When you make a claim, prove it with a benchmark, a customer quote, a real number. Selling less is what gets you cited, and in a market where the buyer is increasingly a model, being cited is the distribution.
