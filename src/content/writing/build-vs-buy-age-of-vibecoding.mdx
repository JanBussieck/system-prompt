For twenty years the build-vs-buy conversation had a comfortable shape. Building cost a lot up front, so buying won on speed. That logic no longer holds, and if your positioning still assumes it, you're losing deals you should be winning.

## What actually changed

Vibecoding didn't make building cheaper across the board. It made *starting* cheap and left *owning* exactly as expensive as it always was. The upfront cost that used to protect your "buy" pitch has mostly evaporated. A competent engineer with a good model can stand up something that looks like your product's core feature before lunch.

So the prospect's internal build advocate now has a working prototype and a good story: we already have it, and it cost us a day. Your job is no longer to argue that building is slow. It's to make the total cost of ownership visible before that prototype ships to production and starts quietly accruing debt.

## The new checklist buyers should be using

Three questions decide it now, and none of them is how long it takes to reach a demo.

The first is time to trust rather than time to demo. How long until this thing is safe in front of a paying customer? Generation gets you to a demo in hours and to trust in months. That gap is your product.

The second is maintenance surface. Every generated feature is a feature the team patches and debugs at 2am. What's the ongoing tax, and who pays it once the person who wrote the prompt has moved teams?

The third is agent compatibility. The build advocate is increasingly not a person but an agent scaffolding a solution. Either the buyer's own tooling reaches for your primitive or it reinvents it badly, and a tool that agents already know how to wire up wins without an argument.

## Selling into a world where "build" is free to start

Pretending building is hard won't work anymore. The move is to shift the comparison from creation to ownership, so the buyer is thinking about the second Tuesday rather than the first afternoon.

Concretely: publish the real maintenance stories. Show the CVE you patched that every DIY implementation is still exposed to. Quantify the edge cases baked into your defaults. Give the buyer's champion the ammunition to say out loud, "yes we could build it, and here's the five-figure annual cost of keeping it alive that the prototype is hiding from us."

Building will keep getting cheaper to start. Owning it won't get cheaper at all, and that's the part worth selling.
