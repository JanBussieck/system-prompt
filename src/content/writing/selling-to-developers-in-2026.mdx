"Sell to developers" used to be concrete advice. You knew who the developer was, what they read, what made them roll their eyes, and what earned their trust. That persona is coming apart, and the marketing playbook built on top of it is quietly going stale.

The cause is simple. Agents gave non-developers a credible on-ramp to technical work. A product manager can now describe a feature, watch an agent prototype it, run it, and form a real opinion about whether the approach is any good. They're not writing the production code. But they are evaluating tools, judging technical quality, and increasingly shaping the buying decision. The person in your funnel is no longer reliably a developer, and the developer in your funnel is no longer working the way they used to.

## Who is "the developer" now?

At least three people wear that label in 2026, and they want different things.

The builder still writes and owns production code. They care about the deep stuff: correctness, performance, the second 80%, whether your abstractions hold under load.

The prototyper is a PM, designer, or founder using an agent to reach a working artifact. They care about time to first success and whether the happy path is genuinely happy, and they judge technical quality by outcome rather than by reading your source.

The agent reads your docs and types literally, picks the tool it can reason about, and never sees your homepage. It cares about legibility and whether the example runs verbatim.

No single message flatters all three. The builder is bored by "ship in minutes." The prototyper is intimidated by a wall of API reference. The agent ignores your narrative entirely. Pretending they're one audience is how positioning goes mushy.

## Does classic DX still count?

Yes, though its center of gravity moves. The old DX gospel was pristine docs, a five-minute quickstart, a generous free tier, thoughtful error messages. Most of that still matters, and some of it matters more than it used to.

Documentation is the clearest case. It used to be a courtesy to a human under deadline. Now it's also the surface an agent reads when deciding whether to pick you, and the thing a prototyper leans on when they can't read the code themselves. Good docs went from a nice-to-have to a distribution channel that serves two of your three readers at once.

What loses value is DX-as-vibe: the clever landing page, the developer in-jokes, the aesthetic signaling that you're "one of us." It flatters the builder and does nothing for the prototyper or the agent. Taste still helps, but it can't stand in for legibility.

## How to sell into a blurred persona

A few working principles:

- Segment by job, not by title. "Building to own" versus "prototyping to evaluate" tells you more than "developer" versus "PM" ever did.
- Make the happy path impossible to get wrong and the deep path real. The prototyper needs the first to succeed, the builder needs the second to exist, and skimping on either loses you half the room.
- Treat docs as product marketing. They're read by the buyer, the builder, and the buyer's agent, so fund them like the growth asset they've become.
- Give the prototyper a way to judge quality without reading code. Live demos, honest benchmarks, visible edge-case handling. They're forming a technical opinion whether or not you help them form it well.

Selling to developers in 2026 means selling to a spectrum of technical confidence, some of it human and some of it not. The useful question isn't how do we appeal to developers. It's which job this person is doing, and which of your three readers is actually in the room. Usually it's more than one.
